The short version

Inanis Wallscape has no servers. Nothing about you is sent to the developer — not ever, not in any form — and there is no account, no analytics, no crash reporting and no advertising identifier.

There is one exception, and it is not ours: the Google Play billing library, which the app uses for its optional one-time purchase, sends its own diagnostic records to Google. That is Google's code doing Google's logging; we never see it and cannot switch it off. It is described under Purchases below, and it is why the app's Play listing says it collects "Purchase history" and "Diagnostics".

The only network traffic it creates is your phone loading the websites you chose, directly from those websites — exactly as if you had typed the address into a browser.

A new install contacts nothing at all. It starts on a clock the app draws itself, out of the app, and it stays there until you give it a website.

It does keep things on your phone: your settings, which page the wallpaper was showing, a small diagnostic log, and a small preview image of each page for the settings screen. All of it sits in the app's own private storage, where other apps cannot read it. All of it is deleted when you uninstall. None of it is ever sent anywhere.

The next section says what each of those things is and why it exists. If you want exact file names, preference keys and manifest entries, they are all in Technical details at the end.

About those preview images

This is the one stored thing that holds website content rather than settings, so it is worth saying plainly: if one of your pages shows a dashboard with personal information on it, a small, low-resolution picture of that dashboard is saved in the app's private storage, so the settings screen can preview each page.

They are taken two ways, and never for pages set to show a picture or nothing. The wallpaper saves one from the page it is already drawing, at most once a minute per page and only while the wallpaper is actually on screen. The settings screen also loads a page itself to make a tile when there is no preview for it yet, or the one there is has aged past six hours — at most six pages each time you open that screen, never in the background and never in power-save mode. A page's preview is deleted as soon as you change what that page shows, and all of them go when you uninstall.

If you choose to send a support email

Nothing is ever sent anywhere on its own. The app's Diagnostics screen has buttons that copy text to your clipboard — the app's settings and state, the page diary, and any reports you filed — so that you can paste it into an email if you decide to ask for help.

Those copies include your site names and their addresses, and — if you have used the in-app sign-in browser — the hostnames it moved between while you signed in, which can include the identity provider your sites use. Hostnames only, never the full addresses. None of them ever includes your saved passwords or any cookie.

What a new install shows before you choose anything

The wallpaper has to show something from the moment it is switched on, and what it shows is a clock drawn by the app itself. It is part of the app, the same way its own screens are: there is no address behind it and nothing is fetched to draw it. If you install Inanis Wallscape, tap "Skip setup" and never open it again, your phone never contacts anybody on its account.

The first-run screen that asks which website you want starts with an empty address box. Underneath it are three examples. The first, "Built-in clock", is that same local page. The other two are ordinary public websites, and tapping one is what loads it — nothing beside that box is fetched until you tap it, and the screen says which of the three leave your phone.

Earlier versions of the app seeded a public clock website instead, so a brand new install made a request to a company its owner had never named. That is no longer the case as of the current version.

The websites you choose to display

When Inanis Wallscape shows a website, your phone contacts that website. The site sees what it would see from any browser: your IP address, which browser engine you are using, and any cookies you already had with it. Inanis Wallscape adds nothing to those requests and does not watch them on your behalf. Those sites have their own privacy policies.

Two parts of the app load a site: the wallpaper itself, and the settings screen, which loads a page once to make the small preview image on its tile when there is no recent one. That is the same request the wallpaper makes, to the same address, and it happens at most six times per visit to that screen — never in the background, and never in power-save mode.

Each site has an optional Block ads and trackers setting, off by default, which refuses to load requests to a built-in list of advertising and analytics domains.

Plain http:// addresses are allowed, because many people point this app at something on their own home network — a Home Assistant, Grafana, Pi-hole or Proxmox dashboard — which cannot have the kind of certificate a public website has. That only ever applies to an address you typed in yourself.

Precision mode (optional, off by default)

Samsung doesn't tell wallpapers which home page you're on. Precision mode lets Inanis Wallscape see only the page number, nothing else.

It can't read your screen, your messages, or your passwords, and nothing leaves your phone.

What exactly does it see? Your launcher already announces the page out loud for screen reader users. Precision mode reads that one short line and takes two numbers out of it:

Page 3 of 5

Inanis Wallscape keeps 3 and 5. That is the whole of it. They are saved on this phone — so a restart returns to the page you were on, and a wrong page can be explained afterwards — and are never sent anywhere.

It cannot read your screen. That is not a promise about how the app behaves; it is how the feature is built. Android is told, in the app's own manifest, that this service does not want screen content, so the system never gives it any. It also asks for no other accessibility ability: it cannot see your taps, read what you type, intercept gestures, or see other apps' windows.

It only hears from your launcher. The app lists the launchers it supports, and when the feature switches on it narrows that to the single launcher you actually use. Nothing from any other app reaches it.

About the warning Android shows. When you turn Precision mode on, Android shows its standard warning that this app can "control your device." Android shows that same warning for every app that uses this feature. Inanis Wallscape's setting is set up so it can't read what's on your screen.

About a day later, Android sends a reminder asking you to review Inanis Wallscape's access. That's routine — Android does it for every app that uses this feature, and the reminder can't be turned off. Opening it or swiping it away both stop it. It comes back if Precision mode is switched off and on again, or after some app updates, so you may see it a few times early on. Keep Precision mode on to keep exact page tracking.

Turning it off. It is off until you switch it on yourself, after a full-screen explanation inside the app. The app gives you a one-tap route back to the same Android setting to turn it off again, and page detection falls back to watching your swipes — nothing else changes.

The lock screen, and the picture for when the app is off

If you ask it to, Inanis Wallscape can set a still picture you choose as your lock screen wallpaper. It does that through Android's own wallpaper system — the same thing that happens when you pick a wallpaper in Android's settings — and only when you ask. Afterwards the app keeps a note of which lock wallpaper the system has, so that if a system update quietly replaces your picture, the app can notice and offer to put it back.

In the same way, when you switch Inanis Wallscape off, it can set a picture you chose as your home wallpaper, so your screen isn't left empty.

If you choose the Your pictures wallpaper, the photos you pick for it are read from your phone and drawn on your home screen — they are never uploaded, and nothing about them leaves the device.

Purchases

Inanis Wallscape offers one optional one-time purchase, "Pro". It is handled entirely by Google Play. The app never sees or stores a payment method, and there is no account to create.

What it keeps is a single yes or no: whether Play said you own it. That is saved on your phone so Pro keeps working at startup and while you are offline.

What Google's billing library sends, on its own

To offer a purchase at all, the app has to include Google's Play Billing Library. That library carries its own diagnostic logging, which sends records about the billing library's operation to Google over an encrypted connection. We did not add it, we cannot turn it off, and none of it comes to us — it goes to Google and is covered by Google's privacy policy.

It is mentioned here because it is the only thing in the app that sends anything anywhere other than the websites you chose, and because Google Play's rules count it as data the app collects even though the developer receives none of it. That is why the app's Data safety listing on Google Play declares Purchase history and Diagnostics rather than "no data collected".

The library's logging runs whenever the app talks to Google Play about purchases. It does not read your settings, your sites, your passwords, or anything else in this document.

Children

Inanis Wallscape is not directed at children and collects no personal data from anyone.

Permissions

Android makes an app declare what it is allowed to do. Inanis Wallscape declares six things, and none of them shows you a permission prompt, because none of them is the kind Android asks you about:

  • Use the internet — to load the websites you chose.
  • See whether you have a working connection — to decide whether to retry a page that failed to load.
  • Set the wallpaper — for the lock screen picture, and for putting your chosen picture back when you switch the app off.
  • Ask Android whether Advanced Protection is on — so the app doesn't offer you Precision mode on a phone where Android will refuse it. This grants no access to any of your data.
  • Google Play billing — for the optional one-time purchase.
  • One permission the app defines on itself — added by a Google library so that an internal message the app sends itself cannot be reached by other apps.

Inanis Wallscape does not ask for permission to ignore battery optimization; it sends you to Android's own battery settings instead.

Deleting your data

Uninstalling Inanis Wallscape deletes everything listed above — settings, saved passwords, cookies and cache, preview images and logs. Android removes the app's private storage along with the app. Clearing the app's storage in Android's settings does the same thing without uninstalling.

Two smaller things worth knowing:

  • The reports you file with the two-finger tap can be cleared from the Diagnostics screen whenever you like. The page diary is a small fixed-size log that overwrites its own oldest entries, and goes when the app does.
  • A picture you chose stays wherever it already lived — the app only ever held a pointer to it. A lock screen or home wallpaper you asked the app to set stays set, because once applied it belongs to Android; you can change it in Android's wallpaper settings.

The Precision mode service configuration

From res/xml/page_accessibility_service.xml:

  • canRetrieveWindowContent="false" — the service is structurally incapable of reading screen content, rather than declining to.
  • accessibilityEventTypes="typeViewSelected|typeViewScrolled" — two event types, and no others. typeViewSelected is what Samsung One UI Home emits; typeViewScrolled is what AOSP Launcher3 derivatives use.
  • No accessibilityFlags at all: no touch exploration, no gesture interception, no flagRetrieveInteractiveWindows, no flagRequestFilterKeyEvents, no ability to see other windows.
  • android:isAccessibilityTool="false", because this is a non-accessibility use of the accessibility API and Android requires that to be stated.
  • notificationTimeout="0", feedbackType="feedbackGeneric".
  • packageNames declares 13 launcher packages, and nothing outside this list is ever in scope:

com.sec.android.app.launcher, com.google.android.apps.nexuslauncher, com.android.launcher3, app.lawnchair, com.teslacoilsw.launcher, net.oneplus.launcher, com.nothing.launcher, projekt.launcher, com.actionlauncher.playstore, com.miui.home, com.microsoft.launcher, ginlemon.flowerfree, bitpit.launcher

When the service connects it narrows that list to the single package that is your current default launcher. If the default launcher cannot be determined, the declared list above stands.

Android's own libraries keep private files in the same sandbox — WebView's storage above, and a baseline-profile marker written by AndroidX ProfileInstaller. Those are the platform's, not the app's, and are likewise local.

Pictures you choose are read through Android's document permission system; the app persists the URI and never copies the image into its own storage.

What the three export buttons contain

Nothing is transmitted automatically; each button copies to the clipboard.

  • Copy diagnostics — device model, Android version, launcher package, the app's settings and live state, and site names with their full addresses.
  • Copy log — page_landmarks.log followed by page_events.log.
  • Copy mismatch reports — the contents of page_mismatches.json.

None of the three reads webwall_secrets.xml, and none contains a cookie.

The Play Billing library's own logging

The one thing in the app that sends anything to anyone other than a website the user chose, and the reason the Play listing declares "Purchase history" and "Diagnostics". Read out of com.android.billingclient:billing:9.1.0 and the shipped dex on 18 November 2026:

  • com.android.billingclient.api.zzdt initialises Google's TransportRuntime, builds a transport for CCTDestination — Cloud Client Telemetry — under the log source PLAY_BILLING_LIBRARY, and sends events through it.
  • The transport and its backend, com.google.android.datatransport, are transitive dependencies of the billing library. Nothing in app/src/main/java refers to them; the app has no code that logs anything anywhere.
  • The destination is Google, over TLS: https://firebaselogging.googleapis.com/v0cc/log/batch and https://firebaselogging-pa.googleapis.com/v1/firelog/legacy/batchlog.
  • It needs no permission the app did not already declare — INTERNET and ACCESS_NETWORK_STATE — which is why it is not visible in the permission list above.

The developer has no access to any of it and no way to switch it off short of removing the purchase from the app.

The built-in page, and the only addresses in the binary

The default page is not a URL. Prefs.DEFAULT_URL is the sentinel webwall://clock, which no component on Android can resolve; WebPageController.loadNow recognises it and renders a string constant with loadDataWithBaseURL(null, …), so the document has no origin, no storage and no way to make a request. It is never passed to loadUrl. The page itself contains no http://, no https://, no src=, no @import and no url( — checked by a test rather than by reading it (BuiltInPageTest).

After that change, two third-party web addresses are written into the app's own code, and both are example buttons on the first-run screen that fetch nothing until tapped:

AddressWhere
https://text.npr.orgthe "News" example
https://wttr.inthe "Weather" example

A third, https://time.is, was the seeded default in earlier versions and is gone. One more address appears as text — https://example.com, the grey placeholder inside the empty address box — and is never loaded. (This counts addresses the app itself can navigate to. Google's own libraries contain their own service endpoints; see the data safety notes in docs/play-listing.md.)

Cleartext HTTP

android:usesCleartextTraffic is permitted because the core use case is self-hosted LAN and VPN dashboards (Home Assistant, Grafana, Pi-hole, Proxmox) which serve HTTP on private addresses and cannot hold a publicly-trusted certificate. It only ever applies to addresses the user typed in.
